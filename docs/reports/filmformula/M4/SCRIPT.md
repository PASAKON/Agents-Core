# THE NIGHT THE BAY STOOD UP (working title) · ILAG F2 pilot · script v1 (29 Oct 2026)

Event: Lituya Bay, Alaska, 9 July 1958 (CMO pick, 29 Oct). Format F2 narrated true disaster, fitted to a one-night event:
~10.3 min (10:17 planned), one English narrator, no lip-sync. Every frame is an AI re-enactment (rule 5).
Real people, as named in USGS / public history: Howard G. Ulrich and his son Howard Jr. ("Sonny", 7), boat *Edrie*;
William A. Swanson and his wife, boat *Badger*; two people aboard the *Sunmore* (not named in PP 354-C, so not named on screen). Nobody on screen speaks;
what they did or saw is narrated as their account, never as invented dialogue (rule 6).
Mix rule (CMO 2026-10-29, after ILAG EP1 B2): narration sits only between SFX peaks, never over a crash, the slide or the wave hit.
Every superlative carries a source line in the description. Facts checked 30 Oct against Miller (1960), USGS PP 354-C, pp. 51-59 (eyewitness accounts) and the abstract; the magnitude 7.8 from the Alaska Earthquake Center (sources range 7.8-8.3; description cites it).

Narration: 949 words (measured) ≈ 8.3 min at 115 words/min + ~2 min of silent spectacle and holds = ~10.5 min.

## 0 · Cold open (0:00-0:42)
*Picture: drone over a quiet green bay. A sharp line runs along the mountainside: dark old forest above, young pale trees below.*
**N:** Look at the trees on this mountain. Above a certain line, the forest is hundreds of years old. Below it, every tree is younger than seventy.
**N:** Something stripped this slope down to bare rock, more than five hundred metres up. It wasn't a fire. It wasn't an avalanche.
**N:** It was water. And on the night it happened, three small fishing boats were sitting in its path.

## 2 · The bay and the three boats (0:55-2:30)
*Picture: the bay from above, T-shaped, glaciers at its head, a narrow mouth to the Gulf of Alaska; the small island in the middle.*
**N:** Lituya Bay is a fjord on the Alaskan coast, eleven kilometres long, walled by mountains and fed by glaciers. Its mouth is so narrow and so fast with tide that fishermen timed their crossings to the minute.
**N:** Inside, it is calm. On the evening of the ninth of July, 1958, that calm brought three boats in to anchor for the night.
*Picture: a small wooden troller, a man and a boy on deck; evening light that never quite goes dark.*
**N:** On the *Edrie* was Howard Ulrich, a fisherman, and his seven-year-old son. They came in at about eight in the evening and anchored in a small cove on the south shore.
*Picture: a second boat near the bay's mouth, a couple on deck.*
**N:** Near the entrance lay the *Badger*, with Bill Swanson and his wife aboard. Close by, a third boat, the *Sunmore*, with two people on board.
**N:** In Alaska in July the sun sets late. At ten at night it was only just going down. The bay was glass. The boats swung slowly on their anchors.
**N:** Nobody aboard knew that the mountains around them were sitting on a major active fault. Or that the water they were floating on had done this before.

## 3 · The quake (2:30-3:24)
*Picture: the Edrie's cabin, a cup sliding on a table; outside, the peaks.*
**N:** At about a quarter past ten, the Fairweather Fault slipped. A magnitude seven point eight earthquake.
*Picture: the mountains shudder; snow and dust lift off the ridges; birds explode from the shore.*
**N:** According to Ulrich's account, the violent rocking of the boat woke him. He went on deck and watched the mountains at the head of the bay shake and heave.
**N:** The shaking went on for minutes. Rock and ice poured off the cliffs. Dust rose into the evening light.
*Picture: the Swansons on the Badger, staring at the shore.*
**N:** On the *Badger*, Swanson was woken by the boat vibrating. By his account, he looked up the bay and saw the glacier rise into the air, jumping and shaking.
*Silence 5 s: wide of the bay, the shaking slowing.*

## 4 · The landslide (3:24-4:18)
*Picture: the head of the bay; a narrow arm called Gilbert Inlet; a cliff face above it.*
**N:** But the earthquake was not the real danger. The real danger was a single cliff above a narrow arm of the bay called Gilbert Inlet.
**N:** The shaking broke it loose. About thirty million cubic metres of rock fell from up to nine hundred metres, straight into the water.
*Picture: the rock mass hitting the inlet; water blown sideways; the glacier front shattering.*
**N:** The water had nowhere to go. It was pushed up and out, against the far side of the inlet, with a force no one had ever measured before.
*Silence 4 s.*

## 5 · The wave (4:18-5:35)
*Picture: the opposite ridge; a wall of white water climbing it, trees vanishing in a line.*
**N:** It climbed the opposite ridge to a height of five hundred and twenty-four metres. Higher than the roof of the Empire State Building. The highest a wave has ever been recorded to climb. It tore away every tree, every root, and the soil beneath them.
*Silence 6 s: the water falls back from the ridge.*
**N:** Then it turned, and came down the bay.
*Picture: night-pale bay; a dark line moving toward the camera, growing.*
**N:** From the *Edrie*, Ulrich watched it come. In his account, it was a straight wall of water from shore to shore, possibly a hundred feet high.
**N:** He had less than three minutes.
*Picture: the anchor winch; a man's hands on it; the chain not moving.*
**N:** He could not get the anchor loose. So he let out all of the chain, started the engine, and faced the wave.
*Picture: the boy in the wheelhouse, holding on.*

## 6 · The ride (5:35-7:23)
*Picture: the Edrie climbing the face of the wave; the chain snaps; the boat thrown upward.*
**N:** The wave lifted the *Edrie* straight up its face. The anchor chain snapped like a thread.
*Silence 5 s: the boat at the crest; below, forest under water.*
**N:** For a moment they were above the treetops of the shore. Then the boat slid down the back of the wave, and the water began to slosh back and forth across the bay, wave after wave.
**N:** Ulrich fought the wheel and kept the bow into each one. The *Edrie* stayed afloat.
*Picture: the bay's mouth; the Badger lifted stern first.*
**N:** Near the mouth, the *Badger* was not so lucky with its position, and yet it was luckier than anyone could believe.
**N:** According to the Swansons' account, the wave picked the boat up and carried it over the spit of land at the bay's entrance, so high that they looked down on the treetops below them.
*Picture: the Badger dropping on the ocean side; the hull breaking; a small skiff.*
**N:** It dropped them into the open sea on the far side. The boat broke apart. The Swansons got into their small skiff and waited in the cold water.
*Picture: open water; a light, then no light.*
**N:** The *Sunmore* tried to run for the open sea, out through the mouth.
**N:** It was swamped by the wave and went down with both people aboard.
*Silence 4 s: the empty mouth of the bay.*

## 7 · The night after (7:23-8:30)
*Picture: the bay at night, full of floating trees, ice and debris.*
**N:** When the water settled, the bay was unrecognisable. Its surface was covered with floating trees, broken ice and wreckage, so thick that a boat could barely move through it.
**N:** For half an hour the water sloshed from shore to shore. Then Ulrich steered the *Edrie* through the logs and out of the bay, at eleven that night. Father and son had survived.
*Picture: night sea; another fishing boat; a skiff; two people pulled aboard.*
**N:** Out at sea, about two hours later, another fishing boat picked up the Swansons, alive.
**N:** Two people died in the bay that night: the two aboard the *Sunmore*.

## 8 · What science learned (8:30-9:39)
*Picture: a small plane over the bay, days later; geologists on the bare slope; the stripped ridge.*
**N:** The next morning, a geologist from the U.S. Geological Survey, Don J. Miller, flew over the bay in a small plane.
**N:** What they found was a line, cut clean across the mountains, as if someone had shaved them. Above it, the old forest. Below it, nothing.
*Picture: a survey diagram style frame: the inlet, the slide, the run-up line, 524 m.*
**N:** Miller later measured the highest point the water had reached: five hundred and twenty-four metres. No wave has been recorded climbing higher.
**N:** It changed how science thinks about tsunamis. A wave this size does not need an ocean. It needs a confined space, and a mountain falling into it.
**N:** And Lituya Bay had done it before. The trees told the story: older trimlines and tree rings pointed to giant waves in 1936, and around 1853.

## 9 · The place today (9:39-10:17)
*Picture: return to the cold-open drone shot; young green trees below the line; the bay calm.*
**N:** Today the forest below the line has grown back, but it is still younger, still lighter, still lower. From the air, you can read the height of that night on the mountain.
**N:** Howard Ulrich and his son rode the largest wave ever recorded, and they came home.
