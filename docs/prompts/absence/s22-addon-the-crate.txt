THE FRAME: a plain crating room, photographed dead-on and perfectly symmetrical, the camera at
chest height and square to the far wall. Bare pale plaster walls, a polished grey concrete
floor with long straight joints running away from the camera, and three warm strip lights in a
row across the ceiling. Nothing decorative. Nothing on the walls.
IN THE MIDDLE OF THE ROOM, DEAD CENTRE, STANDS ONE ENORMOUS WOODEN CRATE. It is the shape of a
wall: very wide, very tall, and almost flat — a great pale timber panel-case, taller than the
room's doorway and more than three times as wide, standing upright on two low battens. Fresh
blond plywood faces, pale timber bracing battens across it in a neat symmetrical lattice, four
broad canvas lifting straps hanging slack down its front, and heavy iron corner brackets. In
the centre of the crate, large and clean, a single GOLD LETTER V is stencilled — a mark, like a
maker's brand, not a word and not a sign.
BEHIND THE CRATE, in the middle of the far wall, there is a NORMAL DOORWAY — an ordinary
single door of ordinary height, closed, painted the same pale colour as the wall. It is
obviously, absurdly too small for the crate standing in front of it. The crate could not have
come through that door and it will never leave through it, and the shot says so simply by
letting both be in frame together at the same time.
NOTHING IN THIS ROOM MOVES except fine dust drifting slowly through the beams of the strip
